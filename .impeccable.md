# Praxis design context

## Users

Developers and AI-tool practitioners using Claude Code, Codex, and GitHub Copilot daily. They open the weekly digest in a browser at the start of a week, read it once, and act on it. 5-10 minutes of attention, typically with coffee, on a laptop. They are technical, skeptical, and allergic to gamified UX.

## Brand personality

Three words: **warm · considered · editorial**.

Reference touchstones (held in mind, not literally copied): the typography of *MIT Technology Review* and *Wallpaper\**, the small-multiples discipline of Edward Tufte's printed work, the data-prose rhythm of *The Economist*'s annual review issues, the editorial restraint of the Linear changelog page.

The aesthetic conveys: "this is a serious, evidence-backed read about you, written by someone who measured everything." It is not a SaaS dashboard. It is not a buzzfeed personality test. It is not a Substack post.

## Aesthetic direction

- **Theme**: light. Cream ground (`oklch(96.5% 0.005 80)`), warm rather than crisp.
- **Primary accent**: terracotta (`oklch(54% 0.13 40)`), used for emphasis on lapses, warnings, low scores - the "this is what needs attention" color.
- **Secondary accent**: deep ink-blue (`oklch(38% 0.06 230)`), used for growth/improvement signals, citations, marginalia - the "this is the considered counterweight" color.
- **Type**: serif display + serif body + sans labels. System-font stack only (`Iowan Old Style → Sitka Text → Hoefler Text → Charter → Cambria → Georgia` for serif; `-apple-system → SF Pro Text → Segoe UI → system-ui` for sans). No web font loads: spec requires self-contained HTML.
- **Layout**: editorial single-column read with intentional marginalia, NOT a dashboard tile grid. Asymmetric where it serves hierarchy.
- **Data viz**: every chart earns its place. Small multiples preferred over single complex visualizations. Radar polygons rejected (notoriously hard to read precisely); replaced with arc-indicator layout for the six dimensions.

## Anti-references

The interface MUST NOT look like:

- **A generic SaaS dashboard** (Stripe, Vercel, Linear chrome). Card grids, mostly grey, big numbers in tiles, generic accent color.
- **A modern editorial blog** (Substack, Medium). Big serif title then plain prose. Looks like an article, not a designed artifact.
- **A personality quiz / MBTI report**. Coloured personality types, cute icons, gamified verdict copy.

These three failure modes are the AI design tells to actively design AGAINST.

## Design principles (derived)

1. **Identity, not chrome.** Each weekly digest should feel like an artifact of this user's specific week, not a generic template with their data poured in. Use the user's actual quotes, evidence, and signal shape as the visual anchor.

2. **Data viz must convey what prose can't.** A chart is justified only when it shows shape, distribution, or trend that prose would belabor. Otherwise: cut it. (This is why the radar polygon was killed: it created a SHAPE impression but its precise values were harder to read than bars.)

3. **Editorial typography over dashboard typography.** Vertical rhythm derived from line-height ratios. Display serif at hero size; body serif at 17-19px; sans only for labels and chrome. Drop caps allowed. Small caps for inline labels. Marginalia in the gutter.

4. **One accent, used rarely.** Terracotta is the emphasis color. The secondary ink-blue is the considered counterweight. Both used sparingly: never as background fills, never on body text, never to "decorate." If a section uses no accent, that is correct.

5. **No dashboard tiles.** Numbers live inline in the narrative ("$15.58, mostly Opus on the ralph review") or as marginalia. The "big number / small label / supporting stats" hero pattern is the AI design tell.

6. **No side-stripe borders.** `border-left: 3px solid X` on cards/callouts is the most overused AI design touch and is banned per the impeccable rules. Use full borders, background tints, leading numerals, or no marker at all.

7. **No gradient text.** Solid colors only for text fills. If emphasis is needed, use weight, size, or color shift.
